WELCOME

You are joining an engineering organization of 143 people inside a company of
412. We build robots that move heavy objects around buildings where humans also
work. That single fact shapes almost every engineering norm described in this
document. When a web service has a bad day, a page loads slowly. When we have a
bad day, a 118 kg machine is doing something unexpected in a room with people in
it.

This document covers how we work. It does not cover what we build; for that,
read the Fleet OS Architecture Overview first, then the HX-200 Courier product
specification, then the postmortem for INC-2024-017. Those three documents in
that order will give you a working model of the system, its shape, and its
failure modes.

DEVELOPMENT ENVIRONMENT

Fleet OS is Python and Go on Linux. Firmware is C++ on a real-time Linux variant.
Perception is C++ and Python with CUDA for the training pipeline.

Everyone gets:
  - A Linux workstation. macOS is supported for Fleet OS work only; firmware and
    perception require Linux.
  - Access to the simulator. This is where most development happens. It models
    robot kinematics, battery behavior, Wi-Fi characteristics, and facility
    layouts, and can replay recorded telemetry from real sites. You can run a
    240-robot Cardinal Foods scenario on a workstation.
  - A slot on the hardware-in-the-loop rigs. There are 14 rigs in Austin. They
    are booked through the internal scheduler and they are genuinely scarce;
    plan ahead.
  - Read access to production telemetry from all customer sites, via the support
    tooling. Write access to anything customer-facing requires a separate
    approval and is audited.

The simulator is good but it is not reality. It did not catch INC-2024-017 and it
would not have; the failure required a specific firmware build talking to a
specific Fleet OS build, and at the time we did not simulate that combination.
Treat green simulator results as necessary, not sufficient.


CODE REVIEW

Every change requires at least one approving review. Changes to the following
require two, one of which must be from a designated owner:

  - Anything in the Charge Scheduler or Traffic Manager. Both are resource
    reservation systems where subtle errors produce fleet-wide emergent
    behavior rather than local failures.
  - Anything in firmware touching motion, e-stop, obstacle detection, or battery
    management.
  - Anything changing an interface between Fleet OS and firmware, in either
    direction.
  - Facility map handling.

Review norms:
  - Reviewers are expected to state what they actually checked, not just approve.
    "Logic looks right, did not verify the threshold math" is a useful review.
    A bare LGTM on a scheduler change is not.
  - If a change alters an assumption another component depends on, say so
    explicitly in the description. The firmware 3.8.1 SoC hysteresis change was
    reviewed correctly and approved correctly, and the reviewers had no reason to
    know that Fleet OS 4.2 compared that value with a strict inequality. Write
    the description so a reader in the other subsystem would notice.
  - Disagreement escalates to the group lead quickly. We would rather have a
    fifteen-minute argument than a slow review.


TESTING AND THE CROSS-VERSION MATRIX

Four tiers, all required to pass before release:

  1. Unit tests. Fast, run on every commit.
  2. Simulator integration tests. Full facility scenarios against the simulator.
     Run on every merge to main.
  3. Hardware-in-the-loop. Real firmware on real compute against simulated
     mechanics. Run nightly and on every release candidate.
  4. Cross-version matrix. Every supported Fleet OS release tested against every
     firmware release inside the twelve-month compatibility window. Runs nightly.

Tier 4 is new. It was established on 2024-11-08 as action item AI-5 from the
INC-2024-017 postmortem, and it is the most important process change this
organization made in 2024. Before it existed, the compatibility matrix was a
document that described which versions were supposed to work together. Nobody
ran it. Fleet OS 4.2.1 and firmware 3.8.1 were both correct, both thoroughly
tested, and had never been executed against one another. They deadlocked 61
robots at our largest customer for most of a Saturday.

The rule that came out of this: a compatibility matrix that is not executed is a
statement of intent, not a test. If you find yourself writing a document that
describes which combinations of things should work, ask why it is not a test.


ON-CALL

Engineers join the on-call rotation after roughly three months, once they have
shipped meaningfully and completed the runbook walkthrough. The rotation is
one week, primary and secondary, across Fleet Software, Firmware, and Platform.

  - Primary responds within 15 minutes for SEV-1, 1 hour for SEV-2.
  - Secondary is backup and handles anything the primary cannot get to.
  - Field Operations (Marcus Ozturk's group) is the first line for customer
    sites and escalates to engineering. You are not the first person on the
    phone with an angry customer, and you should not try to be.
  - On-call is compensated. Rates and comp-time rules are in the On-Call
    Compensation Policy maintained by People Ops. Read it before your first
    rotation; there are provisions for extended incident work that people
    routinely forget to claim.

Before your first shift, read the Fleet Recovery Runbook end to end and walk
through section 4 (charge dock reservation deadlock) with your buddy in the
simulator. That section exists because of INC-2024-017 and describes the exact
diagnostic path that took us two hours and fifteen minutes to find live.


INCIDENT AND POSTMORTEM CULTURE

Postmortems are blameless and they are mandatory for any SEV-1 or any event with
customer-visible impact exceeding 30 minutes. Written within five business days.

Blameless does not mean vague. Read the INC-2024-017 postmortem: it names the
exact code, the exact comparison, the exact firmware behavior, and the exact
process gap, and it names the engineers who owned each remediation. What it does
not do is suggest that any individual should have caught it. Both changes were
correct. The system that was supposed to catch their interaction did not exist.

Two habits we ask for specifically:

  - Record detection method honestly. If the customer told us before our
    monitoring did, write that down in those words. In INC-2024-017 the
    detection gap was 41 minutes and the notification arrived by telephone from
    a shift supervisor. That sentence did more to drive change than any other
    part of the document.
  - When you find a defect, ask where else the same shape of defect lives.
    Action item AI-7 audited every resource reservation in Fleet OS for missing
    expiry semantics and found two more.


ENGINEERING VALUES IN PRACTICE

Fail static. When we do not know what is safe, we stop. Every architectural
choice biases toward the system halting predictably rather than continuing
uncertainly. Fleet OS going down stops task assignment; it does not cause a
single robot to move unsafely.

Firmware has final say. Fleet OS is an optimizer. Firmware is the authority.
Nothing in Fleet OS can override an e-stop, disable obstacle detection, or
command motion outside the certified envelope. This is a certification
requirement and it is not configurable, negotiable, or bypassable for testing.

Leases, not locks. Any resource one robot holds and another might need must have
an expiry. The Traffic Manager learned this early. The Charge Scheduler did not,
and that gap is the entire second half of INC-2024-017. Full unification of the
two reservation systems is tracked for Fleet OS 5.0.

Shared problems get shared implementations. When two components solve the same
class of problem, they share code or the difference is documented as deliberate.

The customer is not our monitoring. If a human at a customer site notices a
problem before an alert fires, that is a monitoring defect and gets tracked as
one.
