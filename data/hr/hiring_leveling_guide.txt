HELIX ROBOTICS — ENGINEERING HIRING AND LEVELING GUIDE
Owner: Priya Raghunathan, VP People Ops
Technical owner: Dana Whitfield, Chief Technology Officer
Last revised: 2024-12-02
Classification: Internal. Compensation bands in section 5 are internal only.


1. PURPOSE

This guide defines engineering levels, what is expected at each, how we
interview, and how promotion works. It applies to the 143 people in engineering
across Fleet Software, Firmware, Perception, Platform and SRE, and Quality.

The 2025 plan assumes 34 net engineering additions.


2. LEVELS

E1 — ENGINEER I
  Early career. Delivers well-defined tasks with support. Learning the domain.
  Expected to ask questions constantly and to ship a real change in week one.
  Typical tenure at level: 12 to 24 months.

E2 — ENGINEER II
  Delivers features independently within an established area. Writes tests
  without being asked. Reviews peers' code substantively. Participates in
  on-call after approximately three months in role.
  Typical tenure: 18 to 36 months.

E3 — SENIOR ENGINEER
  Owns a component or subsystem. Designs solutions, not just implementations.
  Makes tradeoffs visible rather than resolving them silently. Mentors E1 and
  E2. Trusted as a designated owner for two-approver changes in their area.
  This is a terminal level; many excellent engineers stay here indefinitely and
  that is entirely fine.

E4 — STAFF ENGINEER
  Owns a problem area that spans components or teams. Identifies work nobody
  asked for and that turns out to matter. Leads incident response and writes
  postmortems that change how the company operates.

  The reference example: Jae-won Park identified the root cause of INC-2024-017
  by noticing that 61 robots all reported state of charge in a 0.5 percentage
  point band, wrote the postmortem, owned four of its nine action items, and
  then ran the audit of every remaining resource reservation in Fleet OS for
  missing expiry semantics — which found two more defects nobody had asked about.
  That last part is the difference between E3 and E4.

E5 — PRINCIPAL ENGINEER
  Sets technical direction for a discipline. Owns architectural decisions with
  multi-year consequences. External credibility in the domain. Trusted to say no
  to executives.

  Tobias Lindqvist is the reference at this level. His post-incident note in the
  firmware 3.8.1 release record — stating plainly that the change was locally
  correct, that its consequences were outside the scope of what firmware testing
  could have caught, and that the defect was process rather than individual — is
  what E5 judgement looks like under pressure.

E6 — DISTINGUISHED ENGINEER
  Company-level technical authority. No current holders. The level exists so
  that the ladder does not end at E5, and we would rather leave it empty than
  award it loosely.

Management track
  M1 Engineering Manager, M2 Senior Manager, M3 Director, M4 VP. Management is a
  different job, not a promotion from E3. Moving between tracks in either
  direction is normal and carries no penalty.


3. WHAT WE ASSESS

Four dimensions, weighted differently by level.

Technical depth
  Does the candidate understand systems, not just APIs? For firmware and
  perception roles, real-time and embedded experience matters. For Fleet Software,
  distributed systems reasoning matters more than any particular language.

Systems judgement
  Weighted heavily from E3 upward. Can the candidate reason about failure modes,
  about what happens when a component behaves unexpectedly, about the difference
  between a system that fails safely and one that fails unpredictably?

  Our standard probe: describe a resource that one actor holds and another needs.
  Ask what happens if the holder never releases it. Candidates who immediately
  reach for timeouts, leases, or expiry are demonstrating exactly the instinct
  whose absence in the Charge Scheduler caused our worst outage.

Collaboration
  Code review norms here expect reviewers to state what they actually checked,
  and expect change descriptions to flag assumptions other subsystems might
  depend on. Candidates who describe review as approval-granting rather than
  as shared understanding tend to struggle.

Safety mindset
  Assessed for every engineering role, including those that never touch firmware.
  We build machines that move 200 to 450 kg through rooms containing people. A
  candidate who treats safety as someone else's department is not a fit,
  regardless of technical strength.


4. INTERVIEW PROCESS

  1. Recruiter screen                       30 minutes
  2. Hiring manager conversation            45 minutes
  3. Technical deep dive                    60 minutes — a real problem from
                                            the candidate's own past, examined
                                            in depth. No algorithm puzzles.
  4. Systems design                         60 minutes — E2 and above
  5. Practical exercise                     90 minutes — debugging a realistic
                                            failure in a simplified version of
                                            our stack. Candidates may use any
                                            documentation or tooling they like.
  6. Collaboration and values               45 minutes
  7. Debrief and decision

Target: 3 weeks from first screen to offer. 2024 actual median was 24 days.

Principles
  - No whiteboard algorithm puzzles. They do not predict performance in this
    domain.
  - The practical exercise mirrors real work: something is broken, the symptoms
    are ambiguous, and the candidate has full access to tools. What we watch is
    how they narrow the problem.
  - Every panel includes someone outside the hiring team.
  - Debriefs are written before they are discussed, so the first strong opinion
    in the room does not anchor everyone else.
  - Any interviewer may veto. Vetoes require a written reason.
  - We give feedback to every candidate who reaches stage 3.


5. COMPENSATION BANDS

Base salary, Austin. Reno at 96 percent, Rotterdam per local benchmarking. Fully
remote US employees are paid on the Austin band regardless of location.

  E1     115,000 — 140,000 USD
  E2     138,000 — 172,000 USD
  E3     168,000 — 215,000 USD
  E4     205,000 — 262,000 USD
  E5     250,000 — 320,000 USD

  M1     165,000 — 205,000 USD
  M2     195,000 — 245,000 USD
  M3     235,000 — 300,000 USD

Equity at hire scales with level. Refresh grants at the annual review from year
two. Bonus target is 8 percent for individual contributors and 12 to 20 percent
for M3 and above.

We benchmark annually against Austin market data and against comparable robotics
and industrial automation companies rather than against consumer software
compensation. We are candid with candidates that we do not match large tech
company cash compensation at senior levels.

Band placement is not negotiated separately from level. If a candidate belongs at
E4, they are placed on the E4 band; we do not resolve a leveling disagreement by
paying an E3 at the top of the band.


6. PROMOTION

Promotion recognizes that someone is already operating at the next level
consistently. It is not a bet on future growth and it is not a retention tool.

Process
  - Nominations at the March review cycle, with an off-cycle window in September
    for clear cases.
  - The manager writes a case with concrete evidence: specific work, specific
    scope, specific impact.
  - Reviewed by a committee of the group lead, one peer-level engineer from
    another group, and People Ops.
  - E4 and above additionally require CTO approval.

What does not constitute a promotion case
  - Time served
  - A competing offer
  - Volume of work completed at the current level
  - Being the only person who understands a component, which is a problem to fix
    rather than a level to reward

What does
  - Scope that has genuinely expanded, sustained over at least two quarters
  - Work that changed how others operate. The cross-version CI matrix that Deepa
    Nair's team built after INC-2024-017 is the clearest 2024 example: it did not
    ship a feature, it changed the failure surface of the entire product.
  - Judgement demonstrated under pressure, particularly during incidents
  - Mentorship that measurably improved others


7. WHAT WE LOOK FOR, STATED PLAINLY

Engineers who ask what happens when this fails. Our architecture is built around
failing static — when the system does not know what is safe, it stops. That
instinct has to be native.

Engineers who notice when two parts of a system solve the same problem
differently. The Traffic Manager had leases; the Charge Scheduler did not; that
gap deadlocked 61 robots for most of a Saturday at our largest customer. We hire
for the disposition that notices such things before an incident rather than
during one.

Engineers who write things down. Postmortems, design documents, and honest
release notes are how a 143-person organization stays coherent.

Engineers who are comfortable saying they do not know. In a domain where being
confidently wrong can hurt someone, calibrated uncertainty is a technical skill.
